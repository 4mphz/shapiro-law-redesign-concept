The Weather Is Not Always the Whole Explanation

  3. The Weather Is Not Always the Whole Explanation

Ice may result from drainage, refreezing, piled snow, leaking equipment, or incomplete removal. The source and timing of the condition often decide the case.

How New York Law May Apply

New York’s storm-in-progress doctrine generally allows a reasonable period after a storm ends before cleanup is required. Liability may still arise from a pre-existing condition or negligent work that creates or worsens a hazard.

Potentially Responsible Parties

  • Property owners, landlords, and managing agents
  • Commercial tenants with relevant control or contractual duties
  • Snow-removal contractors in appropriate circumstances
  • Other entities that created drainage or runoff hazards

Evidence That Can Make the Difference

  • Certified weather data and temperature history
  • Snow-removal contracts, logs, invoices, salt records, and photographs
  • Drainage patterns, downspouts, roof runoff, and prior complaints
  • The condition’s appearance, footprint, thickness, and location

Defenses We Prepare to Meet

Defendants often invoke storm in progress, deny notice, or claim a contractor handled removal. The timeline, source, contracts, and whether cleanup made conditions worse require close analysis.

Frequently Asked Questions

What if snow was still falling?

The storm-in-progress rule can be significant, but pre-existing ice and negligent efforts may create exceptions depending on the facts.

Why save my shoes?

Footwear condition may become disputed and should not be discarded.

What weather information matters?

Hourly precipitation, temperature, prior melting and refreezing, and the time of snow-removal work can all matter.
